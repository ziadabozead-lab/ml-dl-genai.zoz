The city of Port Halden approved a sweeping plan on Tuesday to rebuild its aging waterfront, a project that officials say will protect thousands of homes from flooding and transform the harbor into a year-round destination. The council voted 7-2 in favor of the plan after nearly six hours of public comment. The total cost is estimated at 1.4 billion dollars, making it the largest public works project in the city's history. Construction is expected to begin next spring and continue for about eight years.
At the center of the plan is a new flood barrier that will stretch almost four miles along the shoreline. Engineers designed the barrier to withstand storm surges up to nine feet higher than the record set during the hurricane that battered the coast eleven years ago. The wall will be built in sections, with removable panels that can be raised when forecasters warn of dangerous weather. Between storms, the panels will be stored in a warehouse near the old ferry terminal, leaving the views of the water largely unblocked.
City leaders say the flood barrier is the most urgent part of the project. Port Halden has flooded four times in the past decade, causing an estimated 600 million dollars in damage to homes, shops, and public buildings. Insurance costs in the low-lying neighborhoods have risen sharply, and some residents have already moved away. Mayor Teresa Alvarado told reporters that doing nothing was no longer an option. She said the city could not keep asking families to rebuild after every storm.
The second major piece of the plan is a redesigned waterfront park that will run along the entire length of the barrier. Landscape architects propose walking paths, bicycle lanes, playgrounds, and a large open lawn for concerts and festivals. A network of shallow ponds and marshes will absorb rainwater and provide habitat for herons, turtles, and migrating ducks. Planners hope the park will draw more visitors to the city and give residents a pleasant place to gather. A public market hall, a small science museum, and a fishing pier are also included in the design.
Supporters argue that the project will bring a wave of economic benefits. A study commissioned by the city estimates that construction alone will create about 9,000 jobs over the eight years of work. After the park opens, new restaurants, hotels, and shops are expected to add several thousand permanent positions. The local chamber of commerce endorsed the plan, saying that the harbor has been underused for far too long. Several business owners spoke at the hearing about how flooding has hurt their sales and forced them to close for weeks at a time.
Not everyone is pleased with the proposal. Critics say the cost is far too high for a city with a modest budget and a history of overspending on large projects. Councilmember Daniel Okoye, who voted against the plan, warned that residents could face higher property taxes for decades. He also questioned whether the estimate of 1.4 billion dollars is realistic, pointing out that similar projects in other cities have gone well over budget. City finance officials responded that the plan includes a reserve fund meant to cover unexpected costs.
Environmental groups have raised a different set of concerns. Some worry that building a long wall along the shoreline could disturb fish nurseries and change the flow of water in the estuary. The city has promised to carry out a full environmental review before any work begins. Engineers say the marshes and ponds in the park design are meant to offset any damage and may even improve water quality over time. A coalition of fishing families has asked to be included in the monitoring process, and the mayor agreed.
Funding for the project will come from several sources. The national government has pledged 550 million dollars through a disaster prevention program, while the state will contribute another 300 million. The remaining money will come from municipal bonds, a small increase in the harbor usage fee, and private donations. A group of local philanthropists has already committed 40 million dollars toward the park and the science museum. City officials said they expect additional gifts once construction gets under way.
The plan also addresses the needs of residents who live closest to the water. Roughly 1,200 households in the Fenwick and Marlow neighborhoods will be offered help to raise their homes or, if they prefer, to sell them to the city at fair market value. A housing office will open next month to answer questions and guide families through the process. Tenants in the affected buildings will receive moving assistance and priority for new affordable apartments planned near the park. Community organizers said they will keep a close watch to make sure the promises are kept.
Transportation is another focus. The current harbor road, which often closes during high tides, will be raised by about six feet and widened to include a dedicated bus lane. A new water taxi service will connect the waterfront with the downtown business district and the university campus across the bay. Planners believe that better transit will reduce traffic and make it easier for visitors to reach the park without a car. The old ferry terminal will be restored and reopened as a transportation hub with a cafe and a small visitor center.
Sustainability is built into many parts of the design. Solar panels will cover the roofs of the market hall and museum, generating enough electricity to power both buildings. Rainwater collected from the park will be used to irrigate the lawns and gardens, reducing the demand on the city's drinking water supply. Lighting along the paths will use efficient bulbs that dim when no one is nearby, protecting the night sky for wildlife. The city hopes the project will serve as a model for other coastal communities facing rising seas.
Scientists who study climate and coastal engineering have watched the debate closely. Several said that Port Halden is right to act now, since sea levels along the coast are projected to rise by more than a foot over the next fifty years. Waiting would only make the eventual solution more expensive and more disruptive. One researcher noted that the removable panel system is flexible, allowing the city to add height later if conditions worsen. Others cautioned that no wall can offer complete protection and that residents will still need emergency plans.
The timeline calls for the first phase to focus on the northern stretch of the shoreline, where flooding has been most severe. Crews will begin by driving steel supports deep into the ground, followed by pouring the concrete base of the barrier. Work on the park will start only after the first section of wall is finished and tested. Officials plan to hold public meetings every three months to report on progress and hear complaints. A website will show live updates, budget figures, and photographs of the work.
After the vote, a crowd gathered outside city hall, some cheering and others holding signs asking for more time to consider the costs. Longtime resident Miriam Castellano said she was relieved that something was finally being done after years of watching the water creep closer to her front door. Another resident, retired teacher Paul Lindgren, said he supports protecting the city but worries that ordinary families will end up paying the bill. Both said they plan to attend the next public meeting.
Mayor Alvarado closed the session by thanking the residents who took part in the long process. She described the plan as an investment in the safety and future of Port Halden, and she promised to keep the public informed at every step. The next major milestone is the environmental review, which is scheduled to be completed by the end of the year. If it is approved, contractors will be invited to submit bids in January. Whatever happens, the decision marks a turning point for a city that has spent years debating how to live with the sea.
